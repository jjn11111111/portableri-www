The concept explores how sound changes, syntax, and morphology operate as interconnected patterns rather than isolated occurrences, allowing linguists to reconstruct ancestral languages. [1 (https://chss.wwu.edu/linguistics/lexicosyntactic-coherence-and-complex-verb-morphology), 2 (https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/books/directions/5-weinreich), 3 (https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/books/9789027258205-cilt.358.02dre), 4 (https://bactra.org/notebooks/linguistics.html), 5 (https://www.scribd.com/document/852850374/Comparative-Method)]
The concept of coherence in historical linguistics branches into three primary areas:

1. The Regularity of Sound Change
Historical linguistics relies on the Neogrammarian principle that sound changes are structurally coherent and operate without exceptions. [1 (https://www.english-linguistics.de/2014/07/16/models-of-language-change/), 2 (https://home.uni-leipzig.de/siebenh/pdf/siebenhaar_2009_14_HSK.pdf), 3 (https://fiveable.me/introduction-humanities/unit-11/historical-linguistics/study-guide/F3txmTkUh5VpSyYe), 4 (https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/books/directions/5-weinreich)]

2. Lexicosyntactic and Typological Coherence
Languages exhibit internal structural harmony, meaning changes in one part of the grammar often trigger proportional changes in another.
	•	Morphological Typology: Researchers analyze how languages integrate morphological material into larger clauses (e.g., polysynthesis).
